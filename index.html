Research

What 62 students told us

An anonymous online survey of students aged 11–18 about sleep, phones and how they feel in lessons.

Method

  • Anonymous online survey, 62 responses.
  • Ages: 11–13 (48.4%), 14–15 (22.6%), 16–18 (29.0%).
  • Eight questions on sleep hours, phone use before bed, where the phone sleeps, staying up late, morning tiredness, concentration and willingness to try a curfew.
  • Percentages come from the survey summary. Student counts are worked out from those percentages.

What the data can’t tell us

  • Not proof of cause. Both late phone use and short sleep are common, but these charts look at each question separately. They don’t show that one causes the other.
  • Self-reported. Students estimated their own sleep and phone use.
  • One group, one moment. 62 students at a single point in time. It isn’t a random sample of all students.
  • Phones aren’t the whole story. About 35% say they rarely or never use their phone before bed, yet only about 15% reach 8 hours. Homework, early starts and other habits matter too.

From findings to tools

  • Few students reach 8 hours → the bedtime calculator counts back from your wake-up time.
  • Phones in the bedroom, and staying up late → the countdown and the 7-day challenge.
  • Most would try a curfew → the challenge turns “maybe” into a week of practice.
  • Hard to see your own habits → the sleep score and sleep debt make them visible.

Science

Why you can’t sleep

What researchers know about teen sleep: the body clock, common sleep disorders, and the studies behind this project.

Please read: this page is for learning, not diagnosis. I’m a student, not a doctor. If sleep problems last for weeks or affect your school, mood or safety, talk to a parent, school nurse or doctor.

Why it’s hard to fall asleep

Teen sleep trouble usually has more than one cause. These are the main ones researchers point to.

A later body clock

During puberty the body clock tends to shift later, so feeling awake at 11pm is often biology, not laziness. Studies link the delay to pubertal stage, though the exact mechanism is still being studied.

Screens and light

Large surveys link using devices near bedtime, and even having one in the bedroom, to shorter and poorer sleep. See the studies below.

Caffeine and energy drinks

Reviews of adolescent studies mostly find caffeine and energy drinks go with shorter sleep. Most of that evidence is survey-based, so it shows a link, not proof of cause.

Early starts

A late body clock plus an early school alarm means a sleep debt that builds up across the week.

Irregular timing

Big weekend sleep-ins shift your clock later, which makes Sunday night and Monday morning harder.

Stress and worry

A busy or anxious mind makes it harder to switch off, and poor sleep makes worry feel bigger the next day.

Common teen sleep disorders

Most tired teens don’t have a disorder, but some do. Here are four that come up often. Numbers are from the studies linked below.

Insomnia

Trouble falling asleep, staying asleep or waking too early, at least a few nights a week, that affects daytime life. One large Norwegian study put adolescent insomnia at roughly 14–24% depending on the criteria used.

Delayed sleep phase

The body clock runs so late that falling asleep before the early hours is very hard, and mornings are painful. In 10,220 Norwegian 16–18-year-olds, 3.3% met the criteria, and over half of them also had insomnia.

Sleep apnea

Breathing repeatedly pauses during sleep. Warning signs include loud snoring, gasping, pauses in breathing, morning headaches, and feeling very tired despite a full night in bed.

Restless legs

An urge to move the legs, often with an uncomfortable feeling, that is worse in the evening and at night and makes it hard to settle.

I did not find reliable teen-specific percentages for sleep apnea or restless legs, so I have left numbers out rather than guess.

The studies

Five pieces of research behind the tips on this site. Each link opens the original paper or a trustworthy summary.

Guidelines · 2016

How much sleep do teens need?

Paruthi et al., Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine. A panel of 13 sleep experts reviewed 864 published articles and recommended 8 to 10 hours per 24 hours for ages 13 to 18. It is endorsed by the American Academy of Pediatrics. This is the target my bedtime calculator uses.

Read the paper
Screens · 2016

Devices at bedtime

Carter et al., JAMA Pediatrics. A review of 20 studies covering about 125,000 children and teens found that bedtime device use or access went with less sleep, poorer sleep quality and more daytime sleepiness. Limit: the studies are observational and the overall evidence quality was rated low.

Read the paper
Screens · 2015

Electronics and sleep in 9,846 teens

Hysing et al., BMJ Open. A survey of Norwegian 16–19-year-olds found that using electronic devices before bed, and heavy daytime screen time, were each linked to getting too little sleep. Most students used a device within an hour of sleeping.

Read the paper
Body clock · 2013

Delayed sleep phase in teens

Sivertsen et al., BMC Public Health. A population study of 10,220 Norwegian 16–18-year-olds. About 3.3% had delayed sleep phase, and it was linked to higher school absence.

Read the paper
School start times · 2020

What happens when school starts later?

Widome et al., JAMA Pediatrics. 455 students in the Minneapolis–St Paul area were followed from grade 9 to 11. Those whose school moved to a later start slept about 43 minutes more per night than students at schools that did not change.

Read the NIH summary

What this means for my project

These studies mostly show links, not proof of cause: teens who use phones late tend to sleep less, but other things could be involved. My survey of 62 students has the same limit, which is why the next step is the 7-day challenge, to test what actually changes when screens go off earlier.

When to ask for help

  • Trouble sleeping most nights for several weeks.
  • Loud snoring, gasping or breathing pauses while asleep.
  • Leg discomfort that stops you settling at night.
  • Sleepiness that affects school, mood or safety, for example when travelling or doing sport.

Start with a parent or carer, your school nurse, or your doctor.

About

About the project

A student project about sleep, phones and what a small change might do.

The problem

Sleep is easy to ignore and hard to fix. Students aged 11–18 generally need around 8 to 10 hours a night, and younger students need more. In my survey of 62 students, only about 1 in 7 reached 8 hours on a school night.

The question

To what extent does phone use in the hour before bed reduce sleep duration and next-day concentration among students, and can a simple curfew change it?

What I did

  1. Surveyed students. An anonymous online survey with 62 responses across ages 11–13, 14–15 and 16–18.
  2. Analysed the answers. I worked out the key percentages and looked at what the data can and can’t show. See the research page.
  3. Built tools. A bedtime calculator, phone-off countdown, sleep score quiz, sleep debt calculator and a 7-day challenge. See the tools.
  4. Designed take-home cards. Six short, printable tips, each with a QR code that links back to this site.

My role

I led this project from the question to the finished tools: I wrote the survey, analysed the responses, and designed and built these tools and this website. Thank you to my school team and to the 62 students who took part.

Limits and next steps

  • The survey shows that short sleep and late phone use are both common. It does not prove that one causes the other.
  • Next, I would run the 7-day challenge with a group and compare sleep and tiredness before and after.
  • I would also ask teachers what tiredness looks like in lessons, to add a second view to the students’ own answers.
  • Finally, I would share the results with the school and ask what it would take to try a school-wide “screens off” reminder.

Privacy and sources

These tools run entirely in your browser. There are no accounts. Quiz answers, sleep hours and challenge ticks stay on your own device, and nothing is sent to me.

Sleep guidance: the American Academy of Sleep Medicine recommends 8 to 10 hours for ages 13 to 18, and more for younger children.
